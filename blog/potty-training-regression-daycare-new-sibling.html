Potty Training Regression After Daycare or a New Sibling

The Week Everything Changed

Two weeks ago your toddler was a potty pro. Then the baby came home, or the first day of daycare happened, and now there are wet pants on the kitchen floor again. Three accidents before lunch. Zero warning.

If that's where you are, here's the short version. Potty training regression after daycare or a new sibling is one of the most common setbacks there is, and the timing varies from child to child. The American Academy of Pediatrics lists both a new sibling and a change in child-care routine among the top triggers for regression. Your kid isn't broken, and neither is your training.

What's different about these two triggers is that they hit at the same time for a lot of families. Baby arrives, parental leave ends, toddler starts daycare. That's three changes stacked on a two-year-old. Of course something slipped.

Why Daycare and a New Baby Hit Potty Training So Hard

Toddlers run on two things: routine and attention. A new sibling takes the attention. Daycare takes the routine. Either one alone can knock a freshly trained kid off balance, and together they're a lot.

The good news is that each trigger has its own fix. So it helps to know which one you're actually dealing with.

A new sibling takes the spotlight

Your toddler watches the baby get carried, changed, fed, and cooed over all day. Diapers suddenly look like the ticket to that attention. Accidents aren't a plan they're making on purpose, but the logic underneath is simple: the baby does this, and everyone loves the baby.

The AAP describes this kind of regression as a normal way for a child to meet an emotional need, not a sign that something is wrong with them. That framing matters. It means the fix is connection, not correction. We walk through the sibling version in depth in our post on potty training after a new baby.

Daycare takes the routine

At home, you knew the wiggle. You knew the 20-minutes-after-juice window. At daycare, there's a different bathroom, a busier adult, a group schedule, and a dozen other kids who need help with their pants too.

Daycare regression can involve an unfamiliar routine, emotions, or both. Your child doesn't know where to go, doesn't want to interrupt playtime, or isn't comfortable asking a new grown-up. If accidents only happen at school, start with our guide to getting your daycare provider on the same page. If the setting is preschool, the preschool regression post covers the classroom side.

First, Rule Out the Physical Stuff

Before treating regression as a behavior problem, check with your child's pediatrician about physical causes. HealthyChildren.org, the AAP's parent site, lists constipation and urinary tract infections as common causes of regression. Big changes make both more likely. Kids hold poop at daycare, drink less water in a new place, and get rushed through bathroom trips.

Signs worth a closer look:

  • Constipation: hard or very large stools, straining, going fewer than three or four times a week, belly pain, or small smears of poop in the underwear.
  • UTI: pain or crying while peeing, sudden urgency with only a dribble, a fever with no obvious cause, or urine that smells unusually strong.

If you see any of these, or if the regression came out of nowhere with no new baby or new school to explain it, call your pediatrician. The AAP's own advice is to check with your pediatrician whenever the reason for regression isn't clear. You can read their guidance directly at HealthyChildren.org. We're coaching, not diagnosing, so when in doubt, make the call.

Our posts on breaking the constipation cycle and potty training and UTIs go deeper on both.

The Two-Week Reset Plan

Once your pediatrician has addressed or ruled out physical causes, you can rebuild a comfortable routine at your child's pace. Your child still has the skill. You're rebuilding the routine around it.

Days 1 to 3: Go back to the clock

Bring back scheduled potty sits, the same way you did in the first week of training. Every 90 minutes to two hours, plus right after waking, after meals, and before leaving the house. Offer a calm reminder and let your child help choose the routine. Don't force a sit if they're upset or in pain.

Keep each sit short, two or three minutes, and keep your voice light. You're not punishing anyone. You're just putting the structure back.

Days 4 to 14: Loosen the clock, keep the wins loud

As the accidents drop, stretch the reminders to every two or three hours and lean on your child's signals again. Stay boring about accidents. A calm "pee goes in the potty, let's clean up" and you move on. Save your real energy for successes, especially the first self-initiated trip after the regression.

Keep the routine familiar, but stay flexible. If training is distressing, discuss a temporary break with your pediatrician. Protective underwear isn't a punishment or a failure.

Some children recover in days or weeks; others need more time and support. There's no guaranteed deadline. If you want the full step-by-step version of this reset, our reset guide for kids who were trained and now aren't lays it out day by day.

If the Trigger Is a New Sibling

The reset plan handles the routine. These handle the attention gap, which is what's really driving the accidents.

  • Ten minutes a day, no baby. Pick a time, give it a name like "our special time," and protect it. Ten minutes of undivided attention does more for the potty than any sticker chart.
  • Give them a job with the baby. Fetching a diaper, picking the baby's outfit, singing during tummy time. Big-kid status feels better when it comes with real responsibilities.
  • Potty check before every feed. Long feeds are when toddlers get forgotten and accidents happen. Make a quick sit part of the setup, right alongside grabbing the burp cloth.
  • Let them be little in safe ways. Baby talk, wanting to be carried, asking for a bottle to play with. Say yes to that stuff. It takes the pressure off the one place you need them to stay big.
  • Skip "big kids don't do that." Being big is exactly what they're worried about right now. Keep accidents neutral and wins celebrated.

If the Trigger Is Daycare

Daycare regression usually fixes itself once home and school run the same playbook. Your job is to hand the staff that playbook.

  • Ask three questions at drop-off. How often do you take kids to the bathroom? Who helps with pants? Where is the toilet from where my child plays? The answers tell you where the gaps are.
  • Write a one-page potty card. Your child's word for pee and poop, their tells, how long sits last, and what they do after. Tape it near the cubbies. Teachers appreciate it more than you'd think.
  • Dress for speed. Elastic waists, no overalls, no belts, no tights. A kid who can't get their pants down in ten seconds will have accidents no matter how ready they are.
  • Bookend the day. A potty sit right before you leave the house and another the minute you get home. Those two trips catch most of the transition accidents.
  • Pack more than you think. Three full changes of clothes, in labeled bags, for at least the first two weeks.

Notice whether accidents cluster around transitions, then share the pattern with your child's caregivers.

What Not to Do

Regression tempts even patient parents into things that make it last longer.

  • Don't punish or shame. Time-outs, lectures, and sighs all teach your child that the potty is a place where they fail.
  • Don't retrain from scratch. A full naked weekend or a brand-new reward system signals that the first round didn't count. It did.
  • Don't compare. Not to the classmate who's dry, not to the older sibling who "never did this." Your kid is handling a big change the only way a toddler can.
  • Don't stack more changes. If you can, hold off on a new bed, a new room, or dropping the nap until the potty settles back down.

When It's More Than a Phase

Most regressions after daycare or a new baby run their course in a few weeks. A smaller number need a pediatrician's eyes. Call if you notice:

  • No improvement at all after four to six weeks of a steady routine
  • Pain, straining, blood, or fever alongside the accidents
  • Wetting that comes with constant dribbling or a sudden big jump in thirst
  • A child who seems withdrawn, fearful, or upset well beyond bathroom moments

These signs can have several causes and deserve medical advice. Don't wait for a training deadline if your child has pain, fever, blood in urine, or unusual thirst.

And the reassuring part, one more time: your child learned this once. The skill is still in there. You're waiting for the rest of their world to feel safe enough for it to come back out, and it will.

Key Takeaways

  • A new sibling and a change in child care are two of the most common regression triggers, and both usually resolve in two to four weeks.
  • Rule out constipation and UTIs first, and call your pediatrician if the regression has no clear cause.
  • Reset with scheduled sits every 90 minutes to two hours for three days, then loosen up as accidents drop.
  • For a new sibling, protect ten minutes of one-on-one time daily and give your toddler a real job with the baby.
  • For daycare, hand staff a one-page potty card, dress for speed, and bookend the day with potty sits.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does potty training regression last after a new baby?

It varies. Some children settle back into their routine within days or weeks, while others need longer. Check with your pediatrician about new regression and seek follow-up if it persists or causes distress.

Should I put my toddler back in diapers when daycare starts?

There isn't one rule for every child. Keep familiar routines where they help, send extra clothes, and discuss a temporary break or protective underwear if your child is distressed. Avoid shame and pressure.

Why does my toddler only have accidents at daycare and not at home?

Because daycare regression is usually about logistics, not ability. Unfamiliar bathroom, busy teacher, no one who knows their tells. Give the staff a written potty card, ask how often they take kids, and dress your child so they can get their pants down fast.

Should I delay potty training if a baby is due or daycare starts soon?

If the change is less than a month away and you haven't started, waiting until a few weeks after things settle usually saves everyone stress. If you're already mostly trained, keep going and expect a short dip rather than a full stop.

Is regression a sign my child is struggling emotionally?

Usually not in a worrying way. The AAP frames regression as a normal way for a child to cope with a big change. It becomes worth a doctor's visit if your child seems fearful, withdrawn, or distressed well outside bathroom moments, or if there's no trigger at all.
